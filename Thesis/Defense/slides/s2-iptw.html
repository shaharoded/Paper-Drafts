- What it adds on top of Firth: the Firth models say which components predict an outcome while adjusting for covariates inside one regression (a conditional association). They do not check whether high- and low-adherence patients are actually comparable. IPTW builds two groups that look alike on baseline characteristics and then compares outcomes between them (a marginal contrast), and that balance can be verified directly. Two designs with different assumptions agreeing in direction is stronger than either alone 

- How it is done: split admissions at the median adherence of each outcome's eligible population (high vs low). A Firth logistic model predicts each admission's probability of being in the high group from its baseline covariates: that is the propensity score. Each admission is weighted by the inverse of the probability of the group it is actually in, so patients who look unlikely for their group count more and the two groups become comparable. Weights are stabilized and trimmed at the 1st and 99th percentiles to stop a few extreme patients dominating. Then a weighted logistic regression per outcome gives β and OR = exp(β); P values FDR-adjusted across outcomes - Balance check: max standardized mean difference across covariates after weighting must be below 0.10, otherwise the estimate is not interpretable. Here all ten outcomes pass (max SMD 0.012 to 0.034) 

- What we got: above-median adherence carries 44% lower odds of 30-day death, 82% lower odds of hyperglycemia and 52% lower odds of severe hyperglycemia, against 20% higher odds of mild hypoglycemia. Severe hypoglycemia does not rise significantly (adjusted P = .096); the other five outcomes show nothing - The asymmetry is the result: the drop in hyperglycemia is about 9× larger on the log-odds scale than the rise in hypoglycemia - Caveat: weighting balances measured covariates only. Illness severity and decisions to limit treatment may remain, so say 'associated with' / 'estimated effect', not proven causation
